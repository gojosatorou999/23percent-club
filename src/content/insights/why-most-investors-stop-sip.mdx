Nobody starts a SIP planning to quit it. You set up the auto-debit, you feel briefly proud of yourself, and for a year or two the number in the app quietly goes up. Then the market has a bad quarter, the number in the app goes down for the first time you've actually watched it happen, and you do the thing almost everyone does: you pause the SIP "until things settle down."

That pause is where most SIPs actually die. Not in a dramatic decision to exit the market — in a quiet, reasonable-sounding pause that never gets un-paused.

## The number was never the problem

Here's the part that doesn't get said enough: the SIP wasn't broken when you paused it. The math underneath it didn't change. What changed was your relationship to a number you'd never had to sit with before — a red number, in your own account, that you caused by doing exactly what you were told to do.

<Callout>
This is loss aversion doing its job. Behavioural economics has a well-documented finding that losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good. Your SIP going down ₹8,000 hurts more than it going up ₹8,000 felt good — even though, mathematically, they're the same event happening in different directions.
</Callout>

The instrument didn't fail you. Your nervous system did exactly what nervous systems do.

## Why the pause feels so reasonable

"I'll restart once the market recovers" sounds like patience. It's actually the opposite of what a SIP is designed to do. The entire mechanism behind rupee-cost averaging is that you buy more units when prices are down and fewer when prices are up — automatically, without you having to decide anything. Pausing during a downturn doesn't protect you from volatility. It removes you from the exact months the strategy was built to use.

Run the numbers yourself: a SIP that runs uninterrupted for ten years and one that gets paused for six months during two separate downturns don't just differ by the six months of missed contributions. They differ by what those specific rupees would have compounded into for the remaining years — which is usually a much larger number than six months of contributions alone would suggest.

## What actually breaks the pattern

Three things tend to work, and none of them are "try harder":

**Automate the decision away from the moment of fear.** A SIP mandate that requires you to actively confirm each month gives your loss-averse brain a monthly veto. A standing instruction removes the veto entirely — the discipline is in the setup, not in monthly willpower.

**Decide your response to a downturn before you're in one.** If you wait until the market is down 15% to decide what you'll do, you're deciding under exactly the conditions that produce the worst decisions. Write down, today, what you'll do if your portfolio drops — then when it happens, you're executing a plan, not making a panicked call.

**Stop checking the number as often as you're checking it.** This sounds glib, but it isn't. The more frequently you check a volatile number, the more loss events you experience, even if the long-term trend is upward. Checking daily manufactures anxiety that checking quarterly simply doesn't.

None of this is about being braver than everyone else. It's about not putting yourself in a position where a predictable, well-studied bias gets to make your financial decisions for you.
