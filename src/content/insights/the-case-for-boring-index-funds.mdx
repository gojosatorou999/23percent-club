An index fund does something almost insultingly simple: it buys every company in an index, in the same proportions, and holds them. No research team, no market calls. So why do so many experienced investors end up recommending them?

## The arithmetic

In 1991, the economist William Sharpe pointed out something that sounds obvious once said. Before costs, all investors together earn the market's return, because together they *are* the market. After costs, the average active investor must therefore earn less than the market, by roughly the amount they pay.

An index fund charging 0.1 to 0.3% a year keeps almost all of the market's return. An active fund charging 1 to 2% has to beat the market by that much every year just to match it.

## The evidence

S&P's SPIVA scorecards regularly find that a majority of Indian large-cap funds underperform their benchmark over five and ten years. The picture is more mixed for mid and small caps, where active managers have had more success, but the large-cap result has been consistent.

<Callout>
This doesn't mean active funds are bad. It means that for the large-cap core of a portfolio, a low-cost index fund is a very hard default to beat.
</Callout>

## The behavioural bonus

Index funds have one more advantage: there's nothing to chase. No star manager leaving, no fund topping this year's charts. That removes the temptation to switch, which is one of the most expensive habits in investing.

## What to check

Look at the **expense ratio** (lower is better), the **tracking error** (how closely it follows the index) and the fund size. Then set up a SIP and let it be boring for a very long time.
