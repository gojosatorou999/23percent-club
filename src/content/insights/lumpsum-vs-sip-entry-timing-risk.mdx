You've probably heard some version of this: "SIP is safer than lumpsum, because it averages out your entry price." It gets repeated so often it sounds like a law of investing. It isn't. It's a simplification that's true in some markets and false in others, and the difference matters more than the slogan lets on.

## What rupee-cost averaging actually does

A SIP spreads your entry across many months. If the market falls after you start, later instalments buy more units at lower prices, which pulls your average purchase price down. If the market only ever goes up, this works against you — every later instalment buys fewer units than the first one would have. Averaging isn't a safety mechanism. It's a bet that markets are more likely to be choppy or falling than steadily rising over your specific investment window.

A lumpsum has no averaging at all. You get exactly the return the market delivers from your entry date forward. That's higher-variance in the short term — a bad entry point genuinely costs you more than a bad SIP start does — but it also means you're never structurally *behind* your own money the way a SIP can leave you when the market simply keeps rising.

## The risk that doesn't get named

Here's the harder truth: the real difference between the two isn't "safe vs risky." It's which specific risk you're choosing to take on.

A SIP trades entry-timing risk for a different risk: the risk that you have a large amount of money sitting outside the market, in cash or a savings account, earning close to nothing, while you slowly feed it in over months or years. If markets rise steadily during that window — which they often do over long enough periods — the SIP investor isn't being prudent. They're paying an opportunity cost for a peace of mind that, in that specific scenario, wasn't buying them anything.

<Callout>
This isn't an argument for lumpsum over SIP. It's an argument against treating either one as automatically safer. The right choice depends on what you're actually trying to manage: entry-point regret, or opportunity cost of cash sitting idle.
</Callout>

## A more honest way to decide

If you have a lumpsum available — a bonus, an inheritance, a matured deposit — the question isn't "which is safer" in the abstract. It's two narrower questions:

**How much would it hurt if the market fell 15% the week after you invested it all?** If the honest answer is "a lot, and I'd probably panic-sell," you have a behavioural reason to spread the entry out, even if it's not the mathematically optimal choice in every scenario.

**How long are you comfortable holding a large amount in cash while you average in?** Every month that money sits uninvested waiting for its turn is a month it's not compounding at all — not a smaller, safer version of compounding. Zero.

Neither answer is wrong. But "SIP is always safer" lets you skip asking either question, and skipping them is the actual risk.
