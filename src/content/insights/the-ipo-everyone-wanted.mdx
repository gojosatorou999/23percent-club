*A note before this one: what follows is a composite scenario, built from a pattern that shows up repeatedly around Indian IPO seasons rather than a single named company's actual results. The point isn't which stock. It's the pattern.*

## The scene

An IPO opens with more demand than shares available. Financial news covers it for a week. Group chats fill up with screenshots of application confirmations. Everyone knows someone who "got allotted." On listing day, the stock jumps, and for a few hours it feels like being early to something.

Some of those buyers sell within the first month, having made a quick gain that felt like validation. Others hold on, watch the price give back the listing-day pop over the following months, and quietly stop mentioning it in the group chat.

Meanwhile, a different investor — no allotment, no listing-day excitement, nothing to screenshot — kept their existing SIPs running through the same window. Nobody asked them how it went, because there was no single dramatic day to ask about.

## Why the comparison is unfair, on purpose

This isn't a story about one investment beating another. It's a story about what each approach was actually *for*.

An IPO allotment is, structurally, a single-stock, single-entry-point bet, no matter how good the underlying business turns out to be over the following decade. Concentration risk doesn't go away because the company is a good one — a great company bought at an inflated listing-day price can still be a poor investment for years, purely on valuation, regardless of how well the business itself performs.

<CalculatorEmbed tool="sip" />

A running SIP, by contrast, isn't trying to time a single entry point at all. It's buying small amounts across many market conditions in many companies (via a fund), which means no single day's euphoria or panic determines the whole outcome.

<Callout>
Neither approach is "wrong." Participating in an IPO you understand, sized appropriately, isn't a mistake. The mistake is the version that shows up almost every season: treating listing-day allotment as a substitute for an ongoing plan, rather than one small, sized position alongside one.
</Callout>

## The pattern worth remembering

What tends to separate the two outcomes over a decade isn't who picked the better company. It's who kept showing up after the excitement wore off. The IPO applicant's story has a dramatic first chapter and often a quiet, disappointing middle. The SIP investor's story has no dramatic chapter at all — which is exactly why it rarely gets told, and exactly why it's worth telling anyway.
